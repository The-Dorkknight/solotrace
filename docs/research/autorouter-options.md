# Better autorouting and auto-placement options for SoloTrace: a research note

## Summary

SoloTrace's present router (heading-aware A* on a 0.635 mm grid, one net at a time, rip-up of blocking nets, wire-link jumpers as a last resort, six shuffled retries) is a classic *sequential maze router with rip-up and reroute*. The literature and the open-source tools point to three upgrades that fit a pure-Python, standard-library-only program on a slow laptop:

1. **Negotiated-congestion routing (PathFinder)** as an alternative router mode. It replaces "rip up whoever is in the way" with "let every net overlap, then raise the price of contested cells until the overlaps go away". It is well documented, order-independent in principle, and small to implement on top of the existing A*. It is the best-evidenced candidate for reducing unrouted nets; whether it also reduces jumpers on a single layer is plausible but not demonstrated in any source I found.
2. **Steiner-first multi-pin nets plus Lienig-style net ordering**, a cheap change to how the existing router is driven, with published support for ascending-size ordering and Hanan-grid Steiner points.
3. **A routability-aware placement cost (HPWL plus a RUDY-style congestion estimate) and a Lam-style adaptive annealing schedule** inside the existing simulated annealer, rather than a wholesale switch to force-directed or min-cut placement, which the sources suggest pay off at scales far above 40 parts.

Everything marked *speculative* below is my own extrapolation to a single-copper-layer board; nothing I found measures jumper counts under any of these algorithms.

## 1. Negotiated-congestion routing (PathFinder)

**What it does.** McMurchie and Ebeling's PathFinder (1995) routes every signal with a best-first/Dijkstra search on a resource graph using the node cost `c_n = (b_n + h_n) * p_n`, where `b_n` is the base cost, `p_n` the *present* congestion (a function of how many signals currently use node `n`) and `h_n` the *history* cost. In the first global iteration `p_n` is 1 "so sharing is free"; in later iterations it "rises gradually with the number of signals using node n", and "each iteration that node C is shared, h_n is increased slightly". The loop condition is literally "while shared resources exist", i.e. convergence is checked by counting overused nodes after each full pass; the paper gives no fixed increment, no explicit update formula and no iteration limit ([PathFinder paper, TU Darmstadt mirror](https://www.esa.informatik.tu-darmstadt.de/archive/twiki/pub/Lectures/AlgorithmenImChipEntwurfDe/router-pathfinder.pdf); summary at [Lafayette CAD apps](https://sites.lafayette.edu/cadapps/main-page/pathfinder-fpga-routing-algorithm/)). The paper also notes that rerouting only the signals that touch congested nodes raises the iteration count but lowers total run time (same source).

**How VPR makes it concrete.** VPR (Betz and Rose, 1997) is "based on the Pathfinder negotiated congestion algorithm": each net is first routed on its shortest path even if that overuses wiring, then "each iteration rips up and reroutes every net", with the cost of a resource "a function of the current overuse of that resource"; the original paper used a maximum of 45 iterations and a bounding-box limit of 3 channels ([VPR, FPL 1997](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~jayar/pubs/betz/fpl97vpr.pdf)). The VPR 6 source shows the schedule: `pres_fac = first_iter_pres_fac` for the first pass, then `pres_fac = initial_pres_fac`, then `pres_fac *= pres_fac_mult` after every failed pass, with `pathfinder_update_cost(pres_fac, acc_fac)` run at the end of each pass and `feasible_routing()` as the stopping test; all non-global nets are rerouted every iteration ([VPR 6 route_breadth_first.c](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/~eddieh/vpr6_dox/d7/db7/route__breadth__first_8c_source.html)). Current VTR defaults are `--max_router_iterations 50`, `--first_iter_pres_fac 0.0` ("congestion is ignored on the first routing iteration"), `--initial_pres_fac 0.5`, `--pres_fac_mult 1.3`, `--acc_fac 1`, `--max_pres_fac 1000`, `--bb_factor 3`, and `--astar_fac 1.2` for the directed search, "values between 1 and 2 are reasonable" ([VTR command-line docs](https://docs.verilogtorouting.org/en/latest/vpr/command_line_usage/)). The VPR-style formulas, as written out by Vansteenkiste et al., are: cost `f(n) = b(n) * h(n) * p(n)`; `p(n) = 1` if capacity > occupancy, else `1 + pf * (occ - cap + 1)`, updated whenever a net is rerouted; `h(n)` starts at 1 and after every iteration except the first becomes `h(n) + hf * (occ - cap)` on overused nodes only ([Connection-based router for FPGAs, Ghent](https://core.ac.uk/download/55869032.pdf)). Note the VPR product form differs from the original paper's `(b + h) * p`; both are in use.

**Convergence and iteration counts in practice.** The only PCB-specific PathFinder tuning document I found (OrthoRoute, a GPU PCB router) uses `pres_fac = init * mult^(iter-1)` with mult 1.10 to 1.35, history `+= hist_gain * overuse` (gain 0.15 to 0.20), board-adaptive caps of 40 to 150 iterations, and an early stop when overuse has not improved for 5 iterations; it warns that multipliers above about 1.5 can oscillate, and that a congestion ratio above 1.0 "can't be routed by tuning alone" ([OrthoRoute tuning guide](https://hub.allspice.io/AllSpiceMirrors/OrthoRoute/src/branch/main/docs/tuning_guide.md)). ACORN, a negotiated-congestion die/package/PCB router, adds decaying congestion and simulated-annealing-style "sensitivity" tuning, stops after `20*log10(N_nets)` violation-free iterations, admits that "convergence is not guaranteed", and reports hundreds to thousands of iterations on 356-net packages; it also states that results "do not depend on net order" because each net sees only the previous iteration's congestion ([ACORN paper](https://danboyne.github.io/ACORN/theory/Acorn_paper.31Jan2025.pdf)).

**Does it reduce unroutable nets versus sequential rip-up?** The FPGA community adopted PathFinder precisely because it reduced the channel width needed to route a design compared with earlier rip-up routers, and the original paper's Theorem 1 gives a delay guarantee, but neither the paper nor VPR reports a head-to-head "unrouted nets" count against a sequential maze router, and ACORN explicitly does not benchmark against one. The negotiated-congestion escape-routing paper by the HKBU group applies the idea to PCB escape routing ([ISQED 2010 listing](https://www.isqed.org/English/Archives/2010/Technical_Sessions/123.html)) but I could not read its results. So the honest statement is: the mechanism is proven and widely reused, the claim that it beats sequential rip-up on routability is the field's working assumption, and I found no quantified single-layer comparison.

**Would it reduce jumpers on a single layer? (speculative)** A jumper in SoloTrace is really a failed negotiation: two nets want the same strip of copper and the loser takes a wire link. PathFinder lets *both* nets keep trying for several iterations with rising prices, so the one with a cheaper detour tends to move first instead of the one that happened to be routed later. That should lower the number of nets that end up with no legal path, and therefore the jumper count, but only if the board has slack; where the crossing is topologically forced (a true non-planar netlist, see section 3) no congestion scheme can remove it. I found no publication measuring this.

## 2. What the open-source and commercial PCB routers do

- **FreeRouting (Java, Alfons Wirtz lineage).** Shape-based rather than gridded: free space is split lazily into convex "expansion rooms" (rectangles, octagons or general polygons for 90, 45 and any-angle modes), searched with textbook A* (`expansion_value + destination_distance`), with rip-up-and-reroute passes whose ripup cost "scales with the pass number", periodic board-score snapshots that can be rolled back ("a coarse approximation of simulated annealing"), recursive shove (`ShoveTraceAlgo`, default depth 20) with fall-back to rip-up, a `PullTightAlgo` smoothing pass, and an optimiser that rips up each item and keeps a change only if it cuts vias or length; nets are routed in DSN file order and the author found shuffling net order across runs beat any single run ([tinycomputers.io analysis of the FreeRouting source](https://tinycomputers.io/posts/the-mathematics-of-pcb-trace-routing.html)). The official settings document confirms a fanout pre-pass (default 20 passes), an autorouter stage with `start_ripup_costs`, via and preferred-direction cost factors, and an optimiser ("postroute") stage ([FreeRouting settings.md](https://github.com/freerouting/freerouting/blob/master/docs/settings.md)); the project README gives no algorithm description ([FreeRouting GitHub](https://github.com/freerouting/freerouting)). A pure-Python standard-library port called `freeroute` exists (grid maze engine with rip-up-and-retry, an exact-geometry engine, an expansion-room engine, shove, and a 45-degree recovery pass) and its author admits it leaves more nets unrouted than the Java JAR on dense boards ([freeroute on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/freeroute/)). SoloTrace is architecturally already a cousin of this; the expansion-room machinery is far more than a few hundred lines.
- **KiCad.** The PNS router is interactive: `SHOVE` is "the actual Push and Shove algorithm", `WALKAROUND` routes around obstacles, `LINE_PLACER` places a single track, `OPTIMIZER` shortens lines, and the API is built around cursor gateways and mouse trails ([KiCad PNS namespace, Doxygen](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/namespacePNS.html)). The user manual describes Highlight Collisions, Walk Around and Shove modes, in which the router "will shove obstacles that can be moved out of the way" ([KiCad 9 PCB editor manual](https://docs.kicad.org/9.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html)). KiCad has no built-in autorouter; the old KiCad 4 one "was not very capable and it was removed", and users export Specctra DSN to external tools ([KiCad forum](https://forum.kicad.info/t/autoroute-is-it-coming-mod-edit-short-answer-is-not-in-the-foreseeable-future-so-nothing-to-see-here/30441)).
- **TopoR and topological (rubber-band) routing.** TopoR decides "which side of each obstacle every connection passes on, and in what order the wires cross each region" first and derives any-angle geometry afterwards, so bundles can be reshaped without rip-up; public descriptions are conceptual only ([vectree.io on TopoR](https://vectree.io/c/topor)). The academic root is SURF (Dai, Dayan, Staepelaere): wires are "zero-width flexible curves with fixed endpoints" on a rubber-band sketch, "spokes" prop the sketch away from pins to make room for width and clearance, routability is tested by cuts ("a sketch is routable if and only if no cut has a flow that exceeds its capacity"), and geometry is produced by an ordered sweep that is a homotopic deformation ([Staepelaere, UCSC-CRL-92-39](https://tr.soe.ucsc.edu/sites/default/files/technical-reports/UCSC-CRL-92-39.pdf); [DAC 1991 SURF paper record](https://bibtex.github.io/DAC-1991-DaiDS.html)). A 2021 package-routing paper shows the key advantage: when nets are routed sequentially on a grid "it becomes increasingly more likely that there will be not enough clearance", whereas a topological router fixes relative order first so "paths can always be inserted between already routed paths"; its Circular Frame method completed all 1000 test environments where A* variants managed only about 210 of 1000 at ten nets, and the authors used Python for both, but no code is released ([Topology for Substrate Routing, arXiv 2105.07892](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.07892)). The topological approach is the one most naturally suited to a single layer, but the geometric realisation stage (spokes, cuts, sweep) is the hard part and I found no open implementation small enough to copy.
- **Electric VLSI.** Ships a maze router ("lays individual wires from point to point"), a river router, a mimic router, a cell-stitching router and a sea-of-gates router, plus experimental parallel ones; the public spec page gives no algorithmic detail ([Electric specifications](https://www.staticfreesoft.com/electricSpecs.html)). The companion textbook does not cover placement or routing algorithms ([Computer Aids for VLSI Design contents](https://rulabinsky.com/cavd)). Not a useful template.
- **Specctra.** Cooper and Chyan's 1989 router, bought by Cadence in 1997; "shape-based", with rip-up-and-retry and push-and-shove passes driven by Do-files, but no public algorithm documentation ([Wikipedia: Specctra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specctra)). Its DSN/SES formats are the only well-specified part.

**Re-implementable in a few hundred lines of Python:** PathFinder on the existing grid (yes, it is a cost-function change plus an outer loop); Lienig's net-ordering rules and Hanan-grid Steiner points (yes); a FreeRouting-style "shove" or expansion-room router (no); rubber-band topological routing (the topological search yes, the geometric realisation no).

## 3. Single-layer-specific tricks

**Planarity.** Deciding which nets can share one layer is the maximum planar subgraph problem, "known to be NP-Complete" (Liu and Geldmacher 1977); a spanning tree trivially keeps at least one third of the edges and the best published simple algorithm guarantees at least 2/5 ([Calinescu et al., SODA 1996](https://users.ime.usp.br/~cris/publ/soda96.pdf)). Even the restricted single-layer planar pin-assignment-and-routing problem is NP-complete ([UCSC-CRL-95-41](https://tr.soe.ucsc.edu/research/technical-reports/UCSC-CRL-95-41)). Marek-Sadowska and Tarng's 1983 paper combines planarity testing with detailed single-layer routing inside an arbitrary region ([UCSB listing](https://vlsicad.ece.ucsb.edu/?q=node/200)), and Ho, Vijayan and Wong give an O(n) feasibility test and an O(mn) planar topological construction for pad nets around a boundary ([IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/planar-topological-routing-of-pad-nets)). None of these treats jumper placement as the objective; I found no paper that minimises jumper count directly for a through-hole board, and tscircuit's `auto_jumper` mode documents only that it "inserts jumper components when needed" ([tscircuit docs](https://docs.tscircuit.com/guides/tscircuit-essentials/single-layer-jumper-autorouting)). *Speculative but cheap:* compute the ratsnest crossing count during placement (SoloTrace already does) and treat it as a lower bound on jumpers; a negotiated router can then be judged against that bound.

**Net ordering.** Lienig's textbook gives three constructive rules: route the net with the larger aspect ratio first (less flexibility), route a net whose pins lie inside another net's bounding box before that net, and route first the net with fewer foreign pins inside its bounding box ([Lienig, chapter 7](https://www.ifte.de/books/eda/chap7.pdf)). For rip-up-and-reroute global routing, the empirical study on CU-GR found that "sorting the nets by their HPWL in an ascending order yields the best results in terms of #vias and TWL"; descending order averaged 5.9 percent more vias; NTHU-Route 2.0 used the opposite (descending bounding box) for its rerouting phase ([Effects of Net Ordering in Global Routing](https://sbmicro.org.br/sforum-eventos/sforum2020/The%20Effects%20of%20Net%20Ordering%20in%20Global%20Routing.pdf)). SoloTrace's "widest first" is a reasonable manufacturing-driven choice, but the evidence favours a containment-then-ascending-HPWL order, with the shuffled retries kept as a safety net.

**Steiner trees for 3 to 6 pin nets.** Hanan showed candidate Steiner points need only lie at intersections of horizontal and vertical lines through the pins, at most n^2 - n points; an RMST costs O(p^2) with Prim ([Lienig, chapter 5](https://www.ifte.de/books/eda/chap5.pdf)). The rectilinear MST is at worst 3/2 of the Steiner optimum (Hwang), and Kahng and Robins' Iterated 1-Steiner (repeatedly add the Hanan point that most reduces MST length) gains "10 to 11 percent over MST lengths", with about 95 percent of the gain in the first round; cost is O(N^3) for the basic version ([Kahng and Robins](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/papers/A_New_Class_of_Steiner_Tree_Heuristics_with_Good_Performance_the_Iterated_1_Steiner_Approach.pdf)). FLUTE is exact up to degree 9 via a 9 MB lookup table and "only 7 percent slower than RMST" ([FLUTE, Chu and Wong](https://eecs.wsu.edu/~daehyun/teaching/2016_EE582/papers/r-flute.pdf)), but the table is overkill here. For 3 to 6 pins, iterated 1-Steiner over the Hanan grid is a few dozen lines and runs in microseconds; for 0/45/90 routing the octilinear variant (Delaunay-triangulate, add 3-pin subtrees shortest first) exists but is heavier ([Lienig, chapter 7](https://www.ifte.de/books/eda/chap7.pdf)). The important practical point is that SoloTrace's "connect each pin to the nearest existing copper" already approximates a Steiner tree in the maze; what it lacks is a *topology plan* before the first segment is laid, which Steiner-first would give.

## 4. Auto-placement

**Simulated annealing.** TimberWolf's cost is half-perimeter bounding-box wirelength plus a squared overlap penalty (with an offset so overlap tends to zero at low T) and a block-length term; moves are displacement, interchange and orientation change, "about 5 to 1" displacements to interchanges, a range-limiter window that shrinks with log T, alpha between 0.8 and 0.95 (near 0.95 while cost falls fast, 0.80 at start and end), and the run ends when cost "has not changed for 3 consecutive stages" ([TimberWolf](https://janders.eecg.utoronto.ca/1387/readings/timberwolf.pdf)). VPR's placer uses a linear congestion variant of HPWL (bounding-box spans divided by channel capacity, weighted by a fan-out factor q(n) from 1 to 2.79), initial T = 20 times the standard deviation of random-move costs, about 10 * N^1.33 moves per temperature (cutting this tenfold costs only about 10 percent quality), the adaptive update alpha = 0.5 / 0.9 / 0.95 / 0.8 for acceptance rate above 0.96 / 0.8-0.96 / 0.15-0.8 / below 0.15, a range limit scaled by `(1 - 0.44 + R_accept)` to "keep R_accept near 0.44", and termination at T < 0.005 * cost / N_nets ([VPR, FPL 1997](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~jayar/pubs/betz/fpl97vpr.pdf)). The 0.44 target comes from Lam and Delosme, who "showed that the ideal run of SA accepts neighbouring states at a rate of 0.44" and adjusted the neighbourhood size to hold it; the Modified Lam schedule (Swartz, Boyan) instead moves the temperature, starting near 1.0, decaying over the first 15 percent, holding 0.44 for 50 percent, then decaying ([Variable annealing length survey, arXiv 1709.02877](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.02877)). Either is directly copyable into SoloTrace's annealer.

**Wirelength models.** HPWL is the "most widely used approximation", MST a "good approximation to Steiner trees", Steiner "computationally expensive" ([NYCU EDA lecture 5](https://tiger.iee.nycu.edu.tw/course/EDAIntro2026Spring/materials/lec5.pdf)). SoloTrace's MST cost is therefore at the accurate end already; the gain is in adding a congestion term, not in swapping MST for HPWL.

**Force-directed, quadratic and min-cut.** Quinn and Breuer's force-directed method treats nets as springs and solves linear equations for zero-force positions; min-cut (Breuer) recursively bisects, and terminal propagation (Dunlop and Kernighan) fixes its blindness to external pins; a genetic placer matched SA quality but "needs more memory" ([NYCU lecture 5](https://tiger.iee.nycu.edu.tw/course/EDAIntro2026Spring/materials/lec5.pdf)). The Kahng-Lienig-Markov-Hu textbook's chapter 4 covers all three but, from the abstract, is framed around chips with millions of cells ([Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-96415-3_4)). Nothing I found claims that analytic or min-cut placement beats SA at 5 to 40 objects; their advantage is scalability, and quadratic placement with a handful of large, oddly shaped through-hole parts needs a legalisation step that would itself be an annealer. My assessment: for SoloTrace's sizes, a plain SA with a better cost and schedule is the right tool, and force-directed placement is worth having only as a fast *initial* placement (seconds, deterministic) to seed SA.

**Routability terms.** RUDY spreads each net's estimated wire area (HPWL times pitch) uniformly over its bounding box, `D(x,y) = sum_n d_n * R(x,y; box_n)` with `d_n = wire_area_n / box_area_n`, is "as good as" a two-bend routing model at predicting demand and almost 11 times faster, and is used inside a force-directed placer by lowering module density where demand is high ([Spindler and Johannes, RUDY](https://scispace.com/pdf/fast-and-accurate-routing-demand-estimation-for-efficient-s4q5dpk379.pdf)). On a single layer, summing RUDY over a coarse grid and penalising cells whose demand exceeds the 0.635 mm track supply (minus pad area) is a cheap congestion estimate; crossing count (already present) and a pin-accessibility term (number of free grid neighbours around each pad, penalising pads boxed in by other pads) complete the picture. The pin-accessibility term is my suggestion, not something I found published for PCBs.

## 5. Practical recommendation

**Rank 1: "Negotiated (PathFinder)" router mode.** Keep the grid, the heading-aware A* and the corner costs. Give every cell an occupancy count, a present-cost factor `pres_fac` and a history cost `h`. Iteration 1: route every net with congestion ignored (`pres_fac = 0`), allowing overlaps; multi-pin nets as now (A* from the growing tree, seeded at zero cost, which is exactly VPR's trick). After each pass, for every overused cell set `h += acc_fac * overuse`; then `pres_fac = 0.5` on pass 2 and `*= 1.3` after that; per-cell A* step cost = `base * h * (1 + pres_fac * max(0, occupancy + 1 - 1))`, with `astar_fac` about 1.2 on the heuristic. Stop when no cell is shared, or after about 30 passes (boards this small should converge in 5 to 15 if OrthoRoute's experience transfers). Any net still overlapping at the end goes to the existing jumper fallback. CPU: each pass is N_nets A* searches, so roughly 10 to 30 times one sequential pass; with the current six-shuffle retry loop dropped (PathFinder is order-insensitive) the net cost is comparable to today's worst case. Risk: oscillation if `pres_fac_mult` is too high (keep it at or below 1.3), and no guarantee of fewer jumpers on truly non-planar nets; measurable only by trying it on the existing test boards.

**Rank 2: "Steiner-first + ordered" driver for either router.** Before routing, build for each net with 3 or more pins an iterated 1-Steiner tree on the Hanan grid of its pins (octilinear distance so it matches the 45-degree moves), split the net into two-pin segments along that tree, and route the segments with the chosen router, shortest segments first. Order nets by Lienig's containment rule, then ascending bounding-box half-perimeter; keep "widest first" only as a tie-break. CPU: negligible (tens of milliseconds for 180 pins). Risk: Steiner points can sit on pads or inside parts, so snap them to the nearest free grid cell; the published ordering results come from multilayer IC routing and may not transfer, so keep the shuffle fallback.

**Rank 3: "Routability placement" for the annealer.** Keep SA but change cost to `w1 * MST + w2 * crossings + w3 * overlap + w4 * RUDY_overflow + w5 * boxed_pads`, where RUDY_overflow sums `max(0, demand - supply)` over a 5 mm grid. Replace the fixed schedule with VPR's acceptance-rate update and range limiter (target 0.44), initial T from the standard deviation of random moves, and stop at T < 0.005 * cost / N_nets. Optionally seed with one force-directed pass (iterate each part to the weighted centroid of its connected parts, then push overlaps apart) instead of a random start. CPU: the RUDY term is O(N_nets * grid cells) per move if recomputed; recompute only the moved part's nets and it stays within a few seconds for 40 parts. Risk: weights need hand tuning; the pin-accessibility term is untested; a force-directed seed collapses all parts to the centre unless overlap repulsion is applied from the first iteration.

**Not recommended now:** FreeRouting-style expansion rooms and shove (too large), rubber-band topological routing (right idea for one layer, but the geometric realisation stage has no small open reference implementation), and analytic or min-cut placement (built for scale SoloTrace does not have).
